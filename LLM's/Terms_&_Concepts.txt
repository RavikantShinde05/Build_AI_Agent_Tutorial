
1. AI Models and LLMs
Artificial intelligence (AI) is the broad field of making computer
systems perform tasks that usually require human-like abilities, such as
understanding language, recognizing images, making predictions, or
solving problems.

An AI model is a system that has learned patterns from data and can
use what it learned to produce an output.

A large language model (LLM) is a type of AI model designed to work
with language. It can understand and generate text, answer questions,
summarize information, and help with writing.

Example: You ask an AI assistant to explain photosynthesis in simple
words. Its language model processes your question and creates an
explanation.

Remember: All LLMs are AI models, but not all AI models are LLMs.
Some AI models work with images, audio, or other kinds of data.
2. Prompt
A prompt is the question, instruction, or information you give an AI
model.

Example:
- Vague prompt: "Tell me about space."
- Clearer prompt: "Explain the solar system to a 10-year-old in five
  short points."
A clear prompt tells the AI what you want, gives useful context, and
describes the format or level of detail you prefer.

Remember: A prompt is the input or instruction you give to the AI.
3. Tokens
Tokens are the small pieces of text that a language model processes.
A token might be a whole word, part of a word, a number, or punctuation.

For example, a sentence is broken into tokens before the model processes
it. The exact way it is split depends on the model.

Tokens matter because:
- Models have limits on how much text they can process at once.
- Many AI services measure usage and charges in tokens.
- Longer prompts and answers usually use more tokens.
Remember: Tokens are the units of text a language model reads and
produces. They are not always the same as words.
4. Context
Context is the information available to an AI model while it is
working on a request.

It may include your current question, earlier messages in the
conversation, instructions, and relevant information from documents or
tools.

Example: You first tell an assistant that you are planning a family
holiday. Then you ask it to suggest activities. It can use the holiday
information you already provided when making suggestions.

A model's context window is the maximum amount of information it can
handle in one interaction, measured in tokens.

Remember: Context is the information the model can refer to during a
task. It is not necessarily permanent memory.
5. Hallucination
An AI hallucination happens when an AI gives information that sounds
believable but is incorrect, made up, or unsupported.

Example: You ask for the opening hours of a local library. The AI
confidently gives hours that the library does not actually use.

Ways to reduce hallucinations include:
- Check important claims against reliable sources.
- Ask the AI to show where information came from.
- Provide trustworthy documents for it to use.
- Verify facts, calculations, and instructions yourself.
Remember: An AI can sound confident and still be wrong.
6. Training Models
Model training is the process of teaching an AI model by giving it
data and adjusting its internal settings so it learns patterns.

A simple comparison is teaching a student: the student studies many
examples, practices, receives feedback, and gradually learns how to
perform a task.

Example: During language-model training, a model processes large
collections of text and learns patterns in language, such as how words
and ideas relate.

Training a large model can require huge datasets, powerful computers,
time, and specialist knowledge.

Remember: Training is how a model learns its capabilities and
patterns from data.
7. Fine-tuning
Fine-tuning means taking an already-trained model and training it
further on a smaller, more focused collection of examples.

Example: A company has a general language model and wants it to
respond in a consistent customer-support style. It may fine-tune the
model using suitable examples of customer questions and good support
replies.

Fine-tuning can help a model become better suited to a particular task,
style, or type of response. It is not always necessary; careful prompts
or retrieval may be enough.

Remember: Training builds a model's learned capabilities;
fine-tuning further adapts an existing model.
8. Inference and Serving
These terms are related, but they mean different things.

Inference is what happens when an already-trained model is used to
produce an answer, prediction, or other output.

Serving means making a model available to users or applications,
often through a website or an API.

Example:
1. A company hosts an AI model on its servers.
2. A user types a question into the company's app.
3. The app sends the question to the model.
4. The model performs inference and generates an answer.
5. The app displays the answer to the user.
Remember: Inference is the model doing the work; serving is the
system that makes the model available.
9. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. It is a method
that lets an AI application find relevant information from a collection
of documents or other sources and provide that information to a language
model when answering a question.

Example: Imagine a company has a folder containing employee
policies. An employee asks, "How many days of annual leave do I get?"

A RAG system can:
1. Search the policy documents for the relevant section.
2. Retrieve the useful information.
3. provide that information to the AI model.
4. Generate an answer based on the retrieved text.
RAG is useful when an application needs to answer questions using
specific documents or information that may change.

Remember: RAG looks up relevant information first, then gives it to
the model to help create an answer. It does not automatically guarantee
that the answer is correct.
10. Tools
Tools are functions or services an AI application can use to do
things beyond generating text.

Examples include:
- A calculator to perform calculations.
- Web search to find information online.
- A calendar service to check appointments.
- A file tool to read or organize documents.
- A database tool to look up stored information.
Example: You ask an AI assistant to calculate the total cost of
several items. The assistant can use a calculator tool rather than
trying to do the arithmetic only by generating text.

A model may decide that a tool is useful and request its use. The
surrounding application runs the tool and returns the result to the
model.

Remember: A model generates responses; tools let an AI application
interact with other services and perform actions.
11. AI Agents
An AI agent is an AI system that can work toward a goal by deciding
on steps and using tools when needed.

A simple chatbot usually responds to a message. An agent may do more:
plan a task, take an action, inspect the result, and decide what to do
next.

Example: You ask an agent to help organize a meeting. Depending on
its permissions, it might check calendars, find a suitable time, and
prepare an invitation for your approval.

Agents vary in how independent they are. Some only suggest actions;
others can carry out approved actions.

Remember: An agent combines an AI model with instructions, tools,
and a process for completing tasks.
12. Model Context Protocol (MCP)
Model Context Protocol (MCP) is a standard way for AI applications
to connect to external tools and information sources.

Think of it as a common connection method between an AI application and
services such as file systems, databases, or other tools.

Example: An AI desktop assistant could connect to an MCP server that
provides access to selected files. The assistant can then search or read
those files when the user asks, subject to the permissions granted.

MCP does not make a model intelligent by itself. It provides a
consistent way for an AI application to access capabilities and
information.

Remember: MCP is a connection standard that helps AI applications
work with external tools and data.
13. Skills and SKILL.md
A skill is a reusable set of instructions that helps an AI agent
perform a particular kind of task.

A file named SKILL.md is commonly used by some agent systems to
describe a skill. It may explain what the task is, when to use the
skill, what steps to follow, and which resources or tools may help.

Example: A "travel planning" skill might tell an agent to ask for
dates and budget, compare suitable travel options, check relevant
details, and present a clear itinerary.

The exact format and behavior depend on the AI platform or agent
framework.

Remember: A tool gives an agent an ability; a skill gives it
reusable guidance for using its abilities to complete a task.
14. Harness
An AI harness is the surrounding software and control system that
helps a model or agent work reliably.

A harness may manage:
- Instructions and prompts.
- Tool connections.
- Permissions and safety checks.
- Conversation or task state.
- Error handling and retries.
- Logging and evaluation.
Example: A coding assistant's harness may let the AI inspect project
files, suggest edits, run tests, read errors, and ask before making
sensitive changes.

Remember: The model is the part that generates or reasons about
outputs; the harness provides the environment and controls around it.
15. Loop Engineering
Loop engineering means designing the repeated process an AI agent
follows while working on a task.

A common loop is:
1. Understand the goal.
2. Decide on the next step.
3. Take an action, such as calling a tool.
4. Observe the result.
5. Decide whether to finish or try another step.
Example: An AI assistant is asked to find a suitable meeting time.
It checks calendars, sees that its first suggestion does not work,
checks another time, and then presents a suitable option.

A well-designed loop needs limits, error handling, and checks so the
agent does not repeat actions endlessly or make unsafe changes.

Remember: Loop engineering is about building a useful and controlled
cycle of action, feedback, and improvement.
16. Computer Use
Computer use means allowing an AI system to interact with a computer
interface in ways similar to a person using a mouse and keyboard.

Depending on its capabilities and permissions, it may be able to:
- View screenshots.
- Click buttons.
- Type into fields.
- Open menus.
- Navigate websites or desktop applications.
Example: You ask an AI assistant to find a document in a web-based
file manager. It may open the file manager, search for the document, and
show you the result.

Computer use can make mistakes. Sensitive actions---such as sending
messages, making purchases, or deleting files---should have suitable
safeguards and human approval.

Remember: Computer use lets an AI interact with graphical
applications, rather than only exchanging text.
17. Evaluation
Evaluation is the process of testing an AI model or application to
see how well it performs.

You might check:
- Accuracy: Is the answer correct?
- Relevance: Does it answer the question?
- Consistency: Does it perform well across similar requests?
- Safety: Does it avoid unsafe or inappropriate actions?
- Speed: How long does it take to respond?
- Usefulness: Does the result actually help the user?
Example: Before releasing a customer-support assistant, a team tests
it with many sample questions and checks whether its answers follow
company policies.

Evaluation should use realistic examples and be repeated when the model,
prompts, tools, or data change.

Remember: Evaluation checks whether an AI system works well enough
for its intended purpose.
18. Security and Privacy
Security is about protecting an AI application and its systems from
unauthorized access, attacks, and misuse.

Examples:
- Keep passwords and API keys secret.
- Give tools only the permissions they need.
- Check who is allowed to access information.
- Protect systems against malicious instructions and unsafe actions.
Privacy is about handling people's personal or confidential
information responsibly.

Examples:
- Do not expose private user information.
- Collect only the data that is needed.
- Limit who can view sensitive information.
- Follow applicable privacy rules and explain how data is handled.
Example: If an AI assistant can read company documents, it should
only access documents that the current user is permitted to see.

Remember: Security protects systems and access; privacy protects
people's information and how it is used.
19. Observability and Cost
Observability means collecting information that helps a team
understand what is happening inside an AI application.

Three common parts are:
- Logs: Records of events, such as a tool call or an error.
- Metrics: Measurements, such as response time, number of
  requests, or failure rate.
- Traces: A record of the steps taken during one request,
  especially when several services or tools are involved.
Cost is the money needed to build and operate the application. Costs
may include model usage, computer servers, storage, databases, and
external services.

Example: A team notices that its AI assistant is slow and expensive.
Logs and traces show that it is searching the same information
repeatedly. The team can improve the process to reduce delays and
unnecessary usage.

Remember: Observability helps explain how the system is behaving;
cost monitoring helps explain what it costs to run.
20. GEO and AEO
These terms describe ways of organizing online content so that search
systems and AI-powered answer services can understand and find it more
easily.

GEO --- Generative Engine Optimization means improving content so it
has a better chance of being discovered, understood, or referenced by
generative AI search experiences.

AEO --- Answer Engine Optimization means structuring content so
answer-focused search systems can identify clear answers to common
questions.

Example: A website that explains how to care for houseplants could:
- Use clear headings.
- Answer common questions directly.
- Explain technical terms.
- Include accurate, useful details.
- Keep information up to date.
GEO and AEO overlap, and neither guarantees that an AI service will show
or cite a particular website.

Remember: GEO and AEO are about making useful content easier for
search and answer systems to understand and surface.
21. AGI and Superintelligence
These terms describe possible future levels of AI capability.

AGI --- Artificial General Intelligence refers to a hypothetical AI
system that can learn, understand, and perform a wide range of
intellectual tasks at a level comparable to humans, rather than being
designed mainly for a narrow set of tasks.

Artificial Superintelligence (ASI) refers to a hypothetical AI
system that would greatly exceed human abilities across most or all
important intellectual areas.

Simple comparison:
- A narrow AI system might be very good at recognizing faces or
  translating languages.
- AGI would be able to handle a broad range of unfamiliar intellectual
  tasks.
- Superintelligence would exceed human performance across a very wide
  range of intellectual tasks.
There is no universally accepted test proving that AGI has been
achieved. Superintelligence remains a hypothetical concept.

Remember: AGI describes broad, human-comparable intelligence;
superintelligence describes intelligence that would greatly exceed human
capabilities.
How the Concepts Fit Together
Imagine building an AI assistant that helps people organize their work.
1. Model training gives the AI its general learned capabilities.
2. Fine-tuning, if needed, adapts the model to a more specific task
   or style.
3. Serving makes the model available through an application;
   inference happens when it responds to a request.
4. A user enters a prompt. The model processes it as tokens and
   uses the available context.
5. The application may use RAG to find relevant information in
   documents.
6. The AI may request tools to perform tasks, such as checking a
   calendar.
7. An agent can plan and carry out several steps. A skill can
   provide reusable instructions, while a harness manages the
   overall process and controls.
8. MCP can provide a standard connection to external tools and
   information. Computer use can let the system interact with
   graphical applications.
9. Loop engineering helps the agent act, observe results, and
   decide what to do next.
10. Evaluation checks whether the system works well. Security and
    privacy protect users and information.
11. Observability helps the team understand the system, while cost
    monitoring helps control operating expenses.
12. GEO and AEO help people make online information easier for
    AI-powered search and answer systems to understand.
A system does not need every one of these components. The right design
depends on what the application is meant to do.
Suggested Learning Order for Beginners
If you are new to AI, learn the subjects in this order:
1. AI models and LLMs.
2. Prompts, tokens, and context.
3. Training, inference, and serving.
4. Hallucinations and evaluation.
5. Tools and AI agents.
6. RAG and MCP.
7. Skills, harnesses, and loop engineering.
8. Security, privacy, observability, and cost.
9. Fine-tuning.
10. Computer use, GEO, and AEO.
11. AGI and superintelligence.
